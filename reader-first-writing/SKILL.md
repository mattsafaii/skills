# Reader-First Writing

Based on Larry McEnerney's argument about why expert writing fails. The core claim, which everything below depends on:

**Writing's job is to change what a reader thinks or does. It is not to express, communicate, or preserve what the writer thinks.** Value does not live in the content. It lives in a specific reader who is not paid to care. The same piece can be valuable to one reader and worthless to another.

The expert's trap: you generate text *horizontally* (left to right, while thinking — you have to, the writing is how you think at this level). But readers consume *vertically*, scanning for value. The language patterns that help you think actively interfere with the patterns a reader uses to read. When interfered with, readers slow down, reread, misunderstand, get annoyed, and stop. In school they couldn't stop (they were paid to care). In the real world they stop in seconds.

## Step 1: Route by mode FIRST — this is the most important step

The framework applies differently depending on what the reader is doing. Misrouting is the main failure: running the proposal checklist on a journal entry, or flattening a piece of fiction into a problem/solution skeleton. Before anything else, ask: **why is this reader reading?**

| Reader is reading to... | Mode | What to apply |
|---|---|---|
| Get their thinking changed | **Persuade** | Full checklist below. Proposals, pitches, cover emails, persuasive essays, business cases, published site/landing copy. |
| Get a task done | **Inform** | Keep value-first framing; DROP "argue, don't explain" — here explanation is the job. READMEs, docs, API references, how-tos, instructions. |
| Feel something | **Move** | Different game. Only "write for a reader, not yourself" carries over. Value is aesthetic, not problem-solving. Fiction, satire, cultural/narrative writing. Do NOT impose problem→solution structure. |
| Nobody — the writer is thinking | **Think** | STOP. Do not apply this skill. This is the horizontal draft and it must stay messy and private. Journals, field notes, notes-to-self, scratch files. |

If unsure, ask the user which mode. Do not guess and proceed — a misrouted edit damages the piece.

The rest of this skill is the **Persuade** mode. For **Inform**, apply only Step 2 and the value-word audit. For **Move** and **Think**, stop here.

## Step 2: Before writing — establish the reader and the problem

Answer these explicitly. If you cannot, the writing has no foundation and polishing it later will not help.

1. **Name the specific readers.** Not "people interested in X." Specific people, specific publication or company, specific community. If you can't name 3–5 actual humans who'd read this, the audience is undefined.
2. **What do they already believe?** The consensus. What's accepted as true.
3. **What do they doubt or worry about?** What's contested, what costs them sleep, what they're exposed to.
4. **What would be valuable to them?** Specifically: what would change a decision, a belief, or a next move. "Interesting" is not valuable. "Changes what they do" is.
5. **What's the instability?** What is wrong, inconsistent, anomalous, or in tension in what they currently believe? No instability = a topic, not a problem. Readers scan for instability; give them stability and they decide there's nothing here.
6. **What's the cost (or benefit)?** If the instability isn't resolved, what does it cost *them*? Or if it is resolved, what do they gain? No cost/benefit = no reason to read.

## Step 3: Draft horizontally

Write to think. The first draft is for the writer, not the reader. Don't apply the rest of the checklist yet — that comes in revision.

## Step 4: Before sending — transform for the vertical reader

1. **Cut the front-loaded explanation.** Background, definitions, topic history, "ever since I was young I've been fascinated by…" — kill it. Open with the problem.
2. **Locate the problem in the reader, not the writer.** Not "I want to study/build X." Instead: "You believe X, but Y is inconsistent with it, which puts Z at risk."
3. **Audit the opening for value words** (see below). If you can't circle 5–10 value words in the first two paragraphs, the reader can't see value yet. Add them.
4. **Strip flow-only transitions from the opening.** *And, because, if, unless* create continuity, not value — and the reader is scanning for tension, not continuity. Fine in body paragraphs.
5. **Argue, don't explain.** Explanation says "here's what's in my head." Argument says "here's why you should change yours." Predict what the reader will doubt at each step and answer it. (Skip this rule in **Inform** mode.)
6. **Fix the lit review / prior-work section,** if any. Catalog ("X said this, then Y said that") is a school habit that proves you read things. Professional version *layers tension*: "X claimed this, but Y complicates it, which suggests the whole approach rests on a flawed premise." It builds the problem; it doesn't list sources.
7. **Match the community's code.** Telling powerful readers they're wrong works — every published paper does it — but only in the community's accepted style. "You missed this" fails. "Your work advanced the field; there's one piece that complicates the picture" works. Read 2–3 recent pieces in the target venue and match tone.
8. **Final test — read the opening as a hostile stranger.** Not "does this make sense to me," but "would I keep reading if I weren't paid to?" If no, nothing downstream matters.

## Value words: the code readers scan for

Readers decide whether a piece is worth their time by scanning for two kinds of signal. Inject these during revision; their absence is usually why an opening reads as "background."

- **Instability words** (signal a problem worth solving): *however, but, although, nonetheless, yet, surprising, paradox, anomaly, inconsistent, fails to, overlooks, despite, contrary, puzzle, breaks down, costs.*
- **Community words** (signal you know who you're talking to): *widely, accepted, established, reported, the consensus, conventional, standard, it is assumed.*

Concrete habit to recommend to the user: print articles or docs from the target community, circle every value-creating word, and build a word list to draw on during revision. Sometimes a stalled piece just needs these injected into a too-stable opening.

## Worked example (Persuade mode)

A follow-up email to a prospective client.

**Before** — everything is about the writer:
> Thanks for chatting last week. I'm a web designer and developer based in LA. I've been building sites for over a decade and specialize in clean, fast, hand-coded sites using vanilla HTML/CSS/JS rather than heavy frameworks. I care deeply about craft and longevity. I'd love to help with your site on a retainer basis — you'd have a dedicated local partner. I think we'd be a great fit.

Clear, organized, friendly — and useless. No problem, so nothing for the reader to want solved. "Vanilla over frameworks" and "50-year longevity" are values to the *writer*; the reader doesn't know or care what's under the hood. Ignoring this email costs the reader nothing.

**After** — problem located in the reader, with a cost:
> Most manufacturers in your space have a site that quietly costs them deals — a slow, dated page that makes a serious company look smaller than it is, while the buyer who landed there is comparing you against three competitors in the next ten minutes. Worse, the people who'd fix it are usually agencies who go dark when you need a change. You said you want a local partner for ongoing improvements rather than a big rebuild — that's the right instinct, but it only works if the partner is actually reachable and fast, which is exactly where the retainer model usually breaks down. Here's what I'd propose: I handle your site as an ongoing relationship, not a project. You email a change, it's live that week.

What changed: the instability is now *the reader's* (their site costs them deals); a second cost names a doubt the reader already had (agencies going dark); the craft shows up as a benefit to them ("faster and cleaner over time"), not as the writer's philosophy; it argues (predicts and answers "does the retainer model actually work?") instead of explaining; and the opening is dense with instability words. Same length, opposite effect — ignoring it now has a cost.

## Diagnostic checklist (when reviewing an existing draft)

Run after Step 1 has confirmed **Persuade** mode. Verdict each item PASS / WEAK / FAIL with a line reference and a quote.

| # | Check | Good | Red flags |
|---|-------|------|-----------|
| 1 | Reader located | Specific community/person named or strongly implied in first 2 paragraphs | "Many people…", "researchers have…", no named reader |
| 2 | Problem-first opening | First 1–3 sentences establish something unstable the reader cares about | Definitions, generalizations, autobiography, "this paper is about" |
| 3 | Instability language present early | At least one instability word in the first ~200 words | Only continuity language (and, also, moreover) |
| 4 | Cost or benefit stated | Reader sees why the instability matters to *them* | Instability noted but consequences absent |
| 5 | Argues rather than explains | Predicts and answers reader doubts | Long stretches of "here is how X works" before any claim |
| 6 | Error over gap | Prior view is wrong / incomplete / overlooks something (in code) | Only "has not been studied" / "little research exists" |
| 7 | Community code present | 5–10 value-signaling words in first 2 paragraphs | Generic prose; no community-specific terms |
| 8 | No martini-glass shape | Specific problem → claim → development | Generalization → narrowing → thesis → broadening |
| 9 | Lit review (if any) builds tension | Citations expose inconsistencies the draft will exploit | Chronological recitation |
| 10 | No school-writing tells | — | "Ever since I was young", "in this paper I will", begin-with-definition |

Lead the report with the 2–3 highest-leverage findings (usually the opening). Don't bury the lede behind every minor item.

## Runnable eval checks

Most can be approximated with grep. Treat them as fast smoke tests, not a substitute for the checklist.

| # | Check | Command | Pass | If fail |
|---|-------|---------|------|---------|
| 1 | Instability word in first ~200 words | `head -c 1500 DRAFT \| grep -iE "\b(but\|however\|although\|anomaly\|anomalous\|inconsistent\|inconsistency\|paradox\|surprising\|contradicts?\|puzzling\|puzzle\|tension\|despite\|nonetheless\|yet\|overlooks\|fails to\|breaks down)\b"` | Match found | Add instability language to opening |
| 2 | No school-writing opener | `head -c 500 DRAFT \| grep -iE "(ever since I was\|in this paper\|the purpose of this\|this essay (will\|argues)\|this article (will\|argues))"` | No match | Cut and reframe as reader-problem |
| 3 | Gap framing not alone | `grep -iE "(has not been studied\|little is known\|few studies\|gap in (the )?literature\|under(-)?studied\|under(-)?explored)" DRAFT` — if matches, then check same paragraph for error/cost language | Either no gap framing, or accompanied by error or cost language | Add error framing or attach a cost |
| 4 | Community words present | `head -c 1500 DRAFT \| grep -iE "\b(widely\|accepted\|established\|reported\|consensus\|conventional\|standard\|assumed)\b"` | Match found | Read 2–3 pieces in target venue, lift vocabulary |
| 5 | First-paragraph problem | Manual: does para 1 name something unstable the reader cares about? | Yes | Rewrite opening to lead with problem |
| 6 | Reader community identifiable | Manual: can a stranger tell who this is for after para 2? | Yes | Name the community or its concerns explicitly |

## Failure modes

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---------|-------|-----|
| Misrouting the mode | Skipped Step 1; ran Persuade checklist on Inform / Move / Think text | Stop. Re-route. Apply only the carry-overs for that mode. |
| Clear and useless | Competence mistaken for value | Locate a problem in the reader; add cost/benefit |
| "New" or "original" used as the value claim | Conflating novelty with value | Replace with anomaly / inconsistent / contradicts — show *which stable belief it unsettles* |
| Opening reads as background or scene-setting | Martini-glass / school model | Cut everything before the first instability marker; start there |
| Premature explanation in para 1–2 | School reflex: prove you understand before claiming | Move all explanation past the point where value + reader-doubt are established |
| Lit review reads as chronological catalog | Treating lit review as proof of reading | Reframe: pair citations to expose tension |
| Gap-only framing ("nobody has studied X") | Writer afraid to say authorities are wrong | Reframe in code: "the prevailing account overlooks…", "the dominant view rests on…" |
| Cost/benefit missing | Writer assumes instability is self-evidently important | Add one sentence: "If this is right, then [consequence the reader cares about]" |
| Reader unclear / "general audience" | Writer hasn't picked an outlet | Stop. Ask which specific outlet or audience. Without it, the skill can't run. |
| Voice flattened after rewrite | Skill over-applied; rewrote too much | Pull back to diagnose-only; propose options not replacements |

## Anti-patterns (do not produce)

- Whole-draft rewrites without being asked
- Critique longer than the original draft
- Findings that don't trace to one of the core premises
- Rewrites that strip the writer's voice (Matt writes plainly; don't add academic register)
- Scolding tone — McEnerney is provocative on purpose; the skill shouldn't be
