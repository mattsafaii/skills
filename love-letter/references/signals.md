# The Five Signals (+ One Secret)

A love letter sends six signals, in order, on one page. Source: Dan Mall's "The 23-Year Mistake" — the technique behind his 90-day Nike close after 23 years of waiting. Each signal has one job. A love letter that skips or mutes one is just another pitch.

The test of the whole thing: **could this letter possibly be sent to anyone else?** If yes, it's not a love letter. Specificity is the entire game.

---

## Signal 1 — "Just like you"

**Job:** get one person's attention (the cocktail-party effect — you can focus on one conversation in a noisy room; the way to pull one person out is to call their name).

**Why it's first and why it's personal:** you address a named human, not a company. "Dear NBA" gets no reply because the NBA can't reply to you. A named SVP/VP/Director can.

**How to execute:**
- Find the person most likely to *hire* the work (not the most senior, not the famous one). Titles that actually buy web design: Head of Product/Design/UX, VP Design, Director of Product Design. Size the company and pick the tier that makes decisions at that size.
- Public digging, never creepy digging — their website, a talk they gave, an interview. Find one true thing that connects them to you: a parallel background, a shared city, an overlapping niche. Mall connected to Abeer because Abeer's parents immigrated from Bangladesh and grew up in Queens; Mall's parents came from Pakistan and the Philippines, first years in the Bronx.
- **One strategic sentence.** Not a biography. One sentence of "we're cut from the same cloth," then move on.

---

## Signal 2 — "We did our homework"

**Job:** prove you understand their business. This is the load-bearing section — the biggest and chunkiest on the page, **at least half the letter**. Don't start building from the top of the page; start here.

**Why it wins work:** shared interests get attention; solving *expensive problems* wins work. A business opportunity with a dollar sign attached, in *their* units, is very hard to ignore.

**The four ingredients, in the recipient's own numbers:**
1. A **problem they own** — their problem, in their language (from their earnings calls, job postings, support forums, reviews).
2. A **stat they recognize** — in their units (revenue, retention, conversion, LTV, load time). Their unit, not yours.
3. A **market opportunity being overlooked** — an adjacent or fast-growing space they're not in. Mall found basketball was a fast-growing sports-betting category and the NBA wasn't resourcing it beyond partnerships.
4. **FOMO** — other companies doing what they're not. Named examples of peers/category players already moving.

**How to execute:**
- Pick *one* product area or opportunity. The letter commits, it doesn't survey.
- Numbers in their units, sourced and dated. Never a fabricated stat — an invented figure kills the letter.
- Close the section with a one-line summary of the whole case (see `scripts.md` — "Fix the foundation once, and every product built on top gets faster, cheaper, and more consistent. Forever.").

## Signal 3 — "Bring 10% to life"

**Job:** make the opportunity *visible*, not just understood. Show what the world looks like if they did this, and position your agency as the way it gets done.

**Why it works:** understanding is the brain; seeing is the eyes. A working artifact converts "interesting" into "I want that." Brad Hussey built the map and the demo of Dan Mall's rebuilt funnel and Dan — a sales teacher who knew every technique being used — still felt "I want that."

**The 10% rule — this is the discipline:**
- Build only the first 10%. Not to be withholding; so that the work is something they **can't do without you** and something **you can't finish without them**. They have to ask "how."
- Show just enough of the "what" and "why" that "how" is the only question left.

**Artifacts that work:** a mockup, a sizzle reel, a sample page, a working snippet, a rebuilt-system demo. For a web design agency the 10% is often a page in their brand — which is also the proof that you can do it.

---

## Signal 4 — "Don't worry: we've been here before"

**Job:** clear the doubts ("is this an amateur with a flash-in-the-pan idea?") with proof that you've done exactly this, at this scale, before.

**Why it works:** after signals 2–3 the only question left in their head should be "how do we get started?" — but only if the doubt is answered first.

**How to execute:**
- **2–3 hand-picked work stories** — not a generic portfolio dump. Each one matches the *scale and stakes* of what you're proposing.
- Match the client type: "We've done similar work for [clients at similar size]." Mall's NBA page showed Nike, The New York Times, Peloton, United Airlines, Marriott — five because the "Starting Five" headline was too good to pass up, but the pattern is the point, not the count.
- One line per proof, credential-first: who, what, at what scale.

---

## Signal 5 — "Let's do this"

**Job:** close. After proof, exactly one question should remain — how do we get started? Answer it with an easy yes.

**How to execute — exactly two questions:**
1. **A closed-ended request** — "Wanna build this together?" Gives the recipient room to say no (that's the point; you want the ask to feel optional so the yes feels chosen).
2. **One crystal-clear next step** — a specific, near-future time: "How's [date ~1 week out] for a chat?" A date that's about a week from today — not too soon, not too far. Make it dynamic so the letter never goes stale.
3. **Contact channels** — email and/or a DM channel. Don't make them work to reach you.

---

## The Secret Sixth — their brand, not yours

**Job:** the most powerful signal of all — "it's like we're already working together."

**Why it works:** our brains treat *continuing* as far easier than *starting*. If the work already looks underway — their palette, their type, their art direction, their tone of voice — the decision to begin feels already made.

**How to execute:**
- Use their color palette, typefaces, art direction, and voice. Research the right elements; source their assets.
- Yes, it costs: fonts to license, maybe a photographer or copywriter to nail the style. **That's a feature, not a bug.** The whole mechanism of the love letter is the feeling of an unreasonable amount of effort spent on one person. The cost IS the message.
- Francine's Spotify page used their gradients, embedded their music players, used their typography. You can't separate that from her getting hired.

**The unifying rule:** the effort itself is the love letter. Every signal is a way of spending unreasonable effort on one person. If any step could plausibly have been sent to a hundred people, it isn't doing its job.
