# The Homework Pass

Signal 2 is at least half the letter, so research is at least half the work. The rule that governs all of it: **everything sourced and dated, nothing fabricated.** An invented revenue figure or a wrong childhood detail kills the letter outright. `Unknown` is a fine answer; a confident guess is the one unrecoverable failure.

This composes with `prospect` (full company recon) and `deep-research` (market). For a love letter you need the *sharp* version of both — not the whole brief, just the four ingredients and the one person.

---

## Part A — The company (the four ingredients)

### 1. A problem they own (in their language)

- **Earnings calls / investor transcripts** — leadership names the problem in their own words; steal the phrasing.
- **Job postings** — what they're hiring for is what they're investing in; a spike of UX/design-system/analytics roles is a told-you-so.
- **Support forums, app-store reviews, help docs, community complaints** — the user-facing pain, verbatim.
- **Their own changelog / product blog** — where they're spending, where they're not.

### 2. A stat they recognize (in their units)

Their units, not yours — revenue, retention, conversion, LTV, churn, CAC, load time. Where it comes from is as important as the number:
- Their public filings/annual report.
- Their own published metrics.
- An industry benchmark their market reads.
- Sourced and dated. The number is the credibility.

### 3. A market opportunity being overlooked

- An adjacent or fast-growing category they're absent from or only partnership-deep in (Mall's find: basketball growing in sports betting, NBA under-resourced there).
- Their competitors/category peers already moving → that's both the gap and the FOMO.
- One opportunity. The letter commits to a single idea; surveying three is a spreadsheet.

### 4. FOMO — other companies doing what they're not

Named examples of peers already acting on the thing you're proposing. "X, Y, and Z all do this; you don't" is the sharpest homework of all — a dollar sign attached to an opportunity is very hard to ignore.

## Part B — The person (public digging, never creepy)

The person who can *hire* the work — sized to the company:
- Enterprise → SVP / Head of Product Experience / VP Design / Director of Product Design.
- Mid-size → VP/Director who owns product or web.
- Small → the owner or marketing lead.

On that person, find:
1. **One quotable line** — a talk they gave, an interview, a post. Quote it exactly. (This is what opened the design-leader story.)
2. **One true shared ground** — background, city, roots, a parallel to Matt. One strategic sentence; not a biography. (Abeer's Bangladesh/Queens ↔ Matt's Pakistan/Philippines/Bronx.)
3. **Their contact path** — email pattern, LinkedIn, DM channel. Where they live, not where you'd prefer to reach them.
4. **The dynamic date** — for the close, ~7 days from send, so the letter never goes stale.

What you must NOT do: turn the person research into a background check. You're proving you understand the *category* and one human connection — not building a dossier.

## Part C — Safaii's unfair advantage

If the target is a web design client, the 10%-to-life artifact and the whole delivery are things **you already do professionally**: a page in their brand. "I made a whole website just for you" beats "I wrote a whole email for you" at a visceral level — everyone can send a text; not everyone can make a website.
