# Rework — principles reference

Every principle from *Rework* (Jason Fried & David Heinemeier Hansson, 37signals,
Crown Business, 2010), in the book's own section order. Each entry: the
principle, its core argument, key supporting detail/examples, and a verbatim line
or two worth quoting. Quote from here rather than reconstructing from memory.

## Takedowns

**Ignore the real world.** "That would never work in the real world" is what
pessimists say about any fresh idea. The real world is where flawed, inefficient,
familiar things win and new ones lose — but it's a mindset, not a law.
> "The real world isn't a place, it's an excuse. It's a justification for not trying. It has nothing to do with you."

**Learning from mistakes is overrated.** Other people's failures are theirs, not
yours — don't absorb the "9 of 10 fail" gloom. Failure teaches what *not* to do;
success teaches what to do again, better. A Harvard study found previously-failed
founders succeed at about the same rate as first-timers (~23%), while
already-successful founders hit ~34%.
> "Failure is not a prerequisite for success." / "Evolution doesn't linger on past failures, it's always building upon what worked."

**Planning is guessing.** Long-term plans are fantasies built when you have the
least information. They put blinders on and kill improvisation.
> "Unless you're a fortune-teller, long-term business planning is a fantasy." / "Working without a plan may seem scary. But blindly following a plan that has no relationship with reality is even scarier." Decide what to do *this week*, not this year.

**Why grow?** Bigger isn't the automatic goal — we don't judge Harvard by how
many campuses it opens. Find the right size and stay there; premature hiring
kills companies.
> "Small is not just a stepping-stone. Small is a great destination in itself." Don't be insecure about staying small if it's sustainable and profitable.

**Workaholism.** The culture worships all-nighters, but working more doesn't mean
caring more or producing more. Workaholics throw brute hours at problems instead
of finding the smart solution, create crises, and make sane workers feel
inadequate.
> "Workaholics aren't heroes. They don't save the day, they just use it up. The real hero is already home because she figured out a faster way to get things done."

**Enough with "entrepreneurs."** The word is a members-only club. Anyone turning
a profit on their own terms qualifies — drop the baggage.
> "Let's just call them starters. … You just need an idea, a touch of confidence, and a push to get started."

## Go

**Make a dent in the universe.** To do great work you have to feel it matters —
that people would notice if you stopped. Craigslist and the Drudge Report (a few
people) reshaped whole industries.
> "If you're going to do something, do something that matters." Feel an urgency: "What you do is your legacy."

**Scratch your own itch.** Build something *you* want to use — you'll know
instantly whether it's any good, with no need for focus groups. (Dyson's bag
vacuum, Vic Firth drumsticks, Nike's waffle sole, Mary Kay.)
> "The easiest, most straightforward way to create a great product or service is to make something you want to use." Best of all, you fall in love with what you're making.

**Start making something.** The idea is the cheap part. "I had the idea for eBay"
is worthless next to actually building it.
> "Ideas are cheap and plentiful. … The real question is how well you execute." Kubrick to filmmakers: get a camera and shoot *something*.

**No time is no excuse.** "Not enough hours" is the most common dodge. You don't
need to quit your day job — find a few hours a week. If you want it badly enough
you make the time.
> "The perfect time never arrives. You're always too young or old or busy or broke or something else." Most people just don't want it badly enough, then hide behind the time excuse.

**Draw a line in the sand.** Great businesses have a point of view, not just a
product. A strong stand attracts superfans and repels others — that's the cost
and the point. (37signals products do *less*; Whole Foods; Vinnie's Sub Shop
closes "when the bread runs out.")
> "If no one's upset by what you're saying, you're probably not pushing hard enough. (And you're probably boring, too.)" / "When you don't know what you believe, everything becomes an argument."

**Mission statement impossible.** A wall plaque about "exceptional service" is
worthless if the experience contradicts it. The recorded "your call is important"
voice is the tell.
> "Standing for something isn't just about writing it down. It's about believing it and living it."

**Outside money is Plan Z.** Most modern (especially service) businesses don't
need it. Raising money means giving up control, "cashing out" trumping building,
no leverage, customers dropping down the totem pole, and massive distraction.
> "Spending other people's money may sound great, but there's a noose attached." / "Before you stick your head in that noose, look for another way."

**You need less than you think.** A long list of "do you really need…?" — ten
people, $500k, a big office, an accountant, fancy business cards. Usually: no,
not now.
> "Great companies start in garages all the time. Yours can too." There's nothing wrong with being frugal.

**Start a business, not a startup.** "Startup" is treated as a magical place
where revenue doesn't matter. Every business obeys the same rules: revenue in,
expenses out, turn a profit.
> "A business without a path to profit isn't a business, it's a hobby." Worry about profit from day one.

**Building to flip is building to flop.** Obsessing over an exit before you've
built anything is backwards — like planning the divorce before the wedding. The
odds of acquisition are tiny, and chasing it makes you emphasize the wrong
things.
> "You need a commitment strategy, not an exit strategy." If you get a good thing going, keep it going — good things are rare.

**Less mass.** You're the leanest you'll ever be right now. Mass — long-term
contracts, excess staff, permanent decisions, meetings, thick process, inventory,
tech lock-ins, long-term roadmaps, office politics — makes you slow to change.
> "The more massive an object, the more energy required to change its direction." Keep mass low so you can pivot fast.

## Progress

**Embrace constraints.** "Not enough time/money/people" forces creativity and
cuts waste. (Sonnets, haiku, Hemingway; Southwest flying one plane model;
Basecamp built by a tiny team across a 7-hour time gap.)
> "Constraints are advantages in disguise. Limited resources force you to make do with what you've got. There's no room for waste." Boxing yourself in prevents bloat.

**Build half a product, not a half-assed product.** You can't do everything well
at once. Cut your ambition in half and do that half excellently.
> "You're better off with a kick-ass half than a half-assed whole." (They cut the book itself from 57,000 to ~27,000 words.) Great ideas you drop will still be there later if they're truly great.

**Start at the epicenter.** Begin with the thing that, if removed, makes the whole
thing cease to exist. A hot dog stand can lose the onions, relish, and cart — but
not the hot dog.
> "Which part of your equation can't be removed? … Then focus all your energy on making it the best it can be."

**Ignore the details early on.** Architects finalize the floor plan before
picking shower tiles. Obsessing over details too soon causes disagreement and
delay; you can't even see which details matter until you start building.
> "Nail the basics first and worry about the specifics later." They sketch with a fat Sharpie, not a fine pen, to stay out of false detail.

**Making the call is making progress.** Postponed decisions pile up and rot.
Decisions are bricks you build on; "we'll decide later" is not.
> "Whenever you can, swap 'Let's think about it' for 'Let's decide on it.'" / "You're as likely to make a great call today as you are tomorrow." And you can correct a mistake later.

**Be a curator.** A great museum is defined by what's *not* on the walls. Keep
removing, simplifying, paring down to the essential, then do it again. (Zingerman's
deli vouches for every olive oil it carries.)
> "It's the stuff you leave out that matters. So constantly look for things to remove, simplify, and streamline."

**Throw less at the problem.** When something's failing, the instinct is to add
people/time/money — which makes it worse. (Gordon Ramsay's first move is always
trimming a bloated menu.)
> "The right way to go is the opposite direction: Cut back." Your project won't suffer as much as you fear — you'll be forced to sort out what matters.

**Focus on what won't change.** Don't build the core around the latest trend.
Build it around things people will want today *and* in ten years. (Amazon: fast
shipping, selection, returns, price. 37signals: speed, simplicity, ease, clarity.)
> "Fashion fades away. When you focus on permanent features, you're in bed with things that never go out of style." No one will ever wish your product were slower or harder to use.

**Tone is in your fingers.** You can buy Eddie Van Halen's exact rig and still
sound like you; he can play a pawn-shop setup and still sound like him. Gear and
tools are a crutch people hide behind instead of doing the work.
> "It's not the gear that matters. It's playing what you've got as well as you can. Your tone is in your fingers." The content is what matters; if you've got nothing to say, fancy equipment won't help.

**Sell your by-products.** Making something always produces something else.
Lumber mills sell sawdust; Wilco filmed its album sessions; Henry Ford turned
wood scraps into Kingsford charcoal. *Rework* itself is a by-product of running
37signals.
> "When you make something, you always make something else. … There's probably something you haven't thought about that you could sell too."

**Launch now.** Once the product does what it needs to, ship it — probably sooner
than you're comfortable with. A two-week deadline forces clarity about what you
actually need. (Basecamp launched without the ability to bill customers; Camper's
"Walk in Progress" store.)
> "When you impose a deadline, you gain clarity. … Build the necessities now, worry about the luxuries later." Don't mistake this for skimping on quality — get to great through iterations.

## Productivity

**Illusions of agreement.** Reports, specs, and diagrams are dead documents that
create false agreement — a hundred people read the same words and picture a
hundred different things. (Alaska Airlines built cardboard mock-ups; Sam Maloof
worked the wood directly.)
> "Get the chisel out and start making something real. Anything else is just a distraction." Remove layers of abstraction; show the real thing.

**Reasons to quit.** Pull your head up and ask hard questions about the work:
*Why are you doing this? What problem are you solving? Is this actually useful?
Are you adding value? Will this change behavior? Is there an easier way? What
could you be doing instead? Is it really worth it?*
> "It's easy to put your head down and just work on what you think needs to be done. It's a lot harder to pull your head up and ask why." Don't throw good time after bad work.

**Interruption is the enemy of productivity.** If you're always staying late, it's
because interruptions wreck the day. Real work happens in the uninterrupted "alone
zone" — like REM sleep, you can't jump straight into it, and any interruption
restarts the clock. Use passive comms (email) over interruptive ones (calls,
meetings).
> "Interruption is not collaboration, it's just interruption. And when you're interrupted, you're not getting work done." Set up an alone-time rule (e.g. no-talk Thursdays).

**Meetings are toxic.** They run on abstractions, convey little per minute, drift,
need prep no one does, have vague agendas, and breed more meetings. A one-hour
meeting with ten people is a ten-hour meeting.
> "The worst interruptions of all are meetings." If you must meet: set a timer, invite as few people as possible, have a clear agenda, start with a specific problem, meet at the site of the problem, and end with a solution and someone responsible for it.

**Good enough is fine.** People get high on complex solutions. Find the "judo
solution" — max result, min effort. Problems are usually negotiable; take the
elevator, don't climb Everest. (Low-fi political attack ads are good enough and
timely.)
> "When good enough gets the job done, go for it. … And remember, you can usually turn good enough into great later."

**Quick wins.** Momentum fuels motivation. Being nine months in with nothing to
show burns people out. Build a steady stream of small victories — something to
announce every couple weeks.
> "Momentum fuels motivation." / "So ask yourself, 'What can we do in two weeks?' And then do it." The longer something takes, the less likely you'll finish it.

**Don't be a hero.** Sunk-cost pride keeps you grinding on a task you estimated at
two hours and are now sixteen into. Hero mode cuts you off from feedback. They
set a rule: if anything takes one person longer than two weeks, bring in others
to look.
> "A lot of times it's better to be a quitter than a hero." The obvious solution might be quitting — don't waste even more time.

**Go to sleep.** Skipping sleep costs you in stubbornness, lost creativity,
diminished morale, and irritability. People who are 10x more effective use
creativity to find 1/10th-effort solutions — and that goes first when you're
tired.
> "Don't be impressed [by sleep-deprivation bragging]. It'll come back to bite them in the ass."

**Your estimates suck.** Humans are terrible estimators — off by a factor of two
even on simple tasks, wildly off on big ones (Boston's Big Dig). The fix: break
the big thing into small things.
> "Instead of one twelve-week project, structure it as twelve one-week projects." Smaller is easier to estimate; if you're wrong, you're wrong by weeks not months.

**Long lists don't get done.** Long to-do lists are guilt trips that collect dust.
Break one list of 100 into ten lists of ten. Don't prioritize with numbers or
labels (everything becomes high-priority) — prioritize visually.
> "Put the most important thing at the top. When you're done with that, the next thing on the list becomes the next most important thing." Only ever have a single next-most-important thing.

**Make tiny decisions.** Big decisions are hard to make and hard to change, and
ego locks you into defending them. Make choices small enough to be effectively
temporary.
> "When you make tiny decisions, you can't make big mistakes." (Polar explorer Ben Saunders: the "huge decision" was so overwhelming his day-to-day rarely extended beyond "getting to that bit of ice a few yards in front of me.") Attainable goals you can nail and build on.

## Competitors

**Don't copy.** Copying skips the understanding underneath — and understanding is
how you grow. The copycat never knows *why* something works, can never keep up,
and is always a step behind.
> "Be influenced, but don't steal." A copy is a faux finish: no substance, nothing to base future decisions on.

**Decommoditize your product.** If you're successful, people will copy you. Make
*you* part of the product — inject what's unique about how you think into what you
sell, and into how you sell, support, and deliver it. (Zappos = Tony Hsieh's
service obsession; Polyface Farm sells a way of thinking.)
> "Pour yourself into your product and everything around your product too. … Competitors can never copy the *you* in your product."

**Pick a fight.** If you think a competitor sucks, say so — being the anti-___
differentiates you and rallies people to your side. (Dunkin' as anti-Starbucks;
Audi vs. old luxury; 7UP the Uncola; "I Can't Believe It's Not Butter.")
> "Having an enemy gives you a great story to tell customers, too. Taking a stand always stands out." You can even pit yourself against a whole industry.

**Underdo your competition.** Forget the arms race of matching feature for
feature. Do *less* — solve the simple problems and leave the hairy ones to
competitors. (Fixed-gear bikes; the Flip camera, which proudly did almost
nothing.)
> "Instead of one-upping, try one-downing. Instead of outdoing, try underdoing." Don't shy away from the fact that your product does less — highlight it, sell it proudly.

**Who cares what they're doing?** Watching competitors becomes an obsession that
breeds anxiety and turns you reactionary. The landscape changes constantly and
it's out of your control.
> "Focus on yourself instead. What's going on in here is way more important than what's going on out there." If you're building "the iPod killer," you've already let them set the rules.

## Evolution

**Say no by default.** Saying yes is easy and piles up; use the power of no to
keep priorities straight. People avoid no because confrontation is
uncomfortable, but dragging things out is worse. "Customer is always right" isn't
gospel — a few vocal customers shouldn't ruin the product for everyone. (ING
Direct says no to credit cards, brokerage, big accounts — to stay simple.)
> "You rarely regret saying no. But you often wind up regretting saying yes." Be honest and explain why; you have to love the product most: "I think you'll love it because I love it."

**Let your customers outgrow you.** Bending the product to one big customer's
growing complexity alienates everyone else — and then that customer leaves and
you're stuck with a bad fit. Adding power-user features scares away the newcomers
who are your real growth.
> "Scaring away new customers is worse than losing old customers." / "Companies need to be true to a *type* of customer more than a specific individual customer with changing needs."

**Don't confuse enthusiasm with priority.** A new idea gives you a rush and you
want to drop everything — but the rush isn't an accurate measure of worth.
> "What seems like a sure-fire hit right now often gets downgraded to just a 'nice to have' by morning." Write the idea down, park it a few days, then judge its real priority with a calm mind.

**Be at-home good.** Some products are "in-store good" — exciting on the shelf
with the most features and the hottest box, disappointing once you use them. Aim
for the opposite: more impressive at home than in the store.
> "You're aiming for a long-term relationship, not a one-night stand." You can't paint over a bad experience with good advertising.

**Don't write it down.** Don't track customer requests in a system. The ones that
matter you'll hear over and over until you can't forget them.
> "Your customers will be your memory. … If there's a request that you keep forgetting, that's a sign that it isn't very important. The really important stuff doesn't go away."

## Promotion

**Welcome obscurity.** Nobody knowing you yet is a gift — make your mistakes,
test ideas, and work out kinks while no one's watching. (Test markets, Broadway
out-of-town tryouts.)
> "Once you do get bigger and more popular, you're inevitably going to take fewer risks." When millions use your product, every change upsets thousands; now you can reason with a hundred.

**Build an audience.** Most companies buy attention with ads — expensive and
unreliable. Instead, earn an audience that comes back to you on its own.
(37signals' Signal vs. Noise blog: 100k+ daily readers.)
> "When you build an audience, you don't have to buy people's attention—they give it to you." Speak, write, blog, share something valuable, and the right people will already be listening when you need them.

**Out-teach your competition.** You can't outspend big competitors — but you can
out-teach them, because teaching never occurs to most businesses and big
companies are too secrecy-obsessed to do it. (Hoefler type foundry; Etsy
workshops; Gary Vaynerchuk's wine videos.)
> "Teaching is something individuals and small companies can do that bigger competitors can't." Teaching earns trust and loyalty an ad never will.

**Emulate chefs.** Famous chefs (Batali, Child, Pepin) are famous *because* they
publish their recipes and show their techniques on TV. A recipe is far easier to
copy than a whole business — and it still doesn't put them out of business.
> "As a business owner, you should share everything you know too." What's your cookbook? "This book is our cookbook. What's yours?"

**Go behind the scenes.** Give people a backstage pass to how your business works.
Even "boring" jobs fascinate (Deadliest Catch, Unwrapped). People are curious how
things are made.
> "Letting people behind the curtain changes your relationship with them. They'll feel a bond with you and see you as human beings instead of a faceless company."

**Nobody likes plastic flowers.** The business world is full of stiff
"professionals" trying to seem perfect. Imperfections are real and people respond
to real. (The Japanese principle of *wabi-sabi*: character over a shiny facade.)
> "Don't be afraid to show your flaws. … You might not seem as professional, but you will seem a lot more genuine." Leave the poetry in what you make.

**Press releases are spam.** A press release is a generic pitch blasted to
hundreds of strangers — tired, formulaic, ignored. To get attention you have to
stand out, not do exactly what everyone else does.
> "Instead, call someone. Write a personal note." Pitch a specific journalist who covered something similar, with passion. "Be remarkable. Stand out. Be unforgettable."

**Forget about the Wall Street Journal.** Pitching Time, Forbes, NYT, WSJ is
nearly impossible and they won't care — you're not big enough yet. Trade
publications and niche bloggers have a far lower barrier and drive more direct
activity. (37signals got more from Daring Fireball / Lifehacker than from Wired or
Time.)
> "Stories that start on the fringe can go mainstream quickly." Niche media beats mass media for a small business.

**Drug dealers get it right.** Dealers give a free taste because they know the
product is good enough to bring you back paying. Make your product
bite-size-easy to try, free, with no big investment of money or time.
> "Be confident in what you're offering. … If you're not confident about that, you haven't created a strong enough product." (Bakeries, test-drives, free software trials.)

**Marketing is not a department.** Marketing isn't a team or a budget line — it's
the sum of everything you do. Every phone call, email, error message,
after-dinner mint, and invoice is marketing.
> "You cannot not market. … Marketing isn't just a few individual events. It's the sum total of everything you do." 24/7/365, everyone does it.

**The myth of the overnight sensation.** You won't be a big hit right away; no one
cares about you yet. "Overnight successes" almost always spent years grinding
first. Don't waste money on a PR firm before you're ready.
> "Trade the dream of overnight success for slow, measured growth." Great brands (Starbucks, Nike, Amazon, Google) became great over time, not from a launch-day PR push.

## Hiring

**Do it yourself first.** Never hire for a job until you've tried doing it
yourself. Then you understand the work, can write a real job description, ask the
right interview questions, and manage well. (37signals did its own support and
sysadmin work for years first.)
> "What you give up in initial execution will be repaid many times over by the wisdom you gain." You should want to be intimately involved in all aspects of your business.

**Hire when it hurts.** Don't hire for pleasure — hire to kill real, sustained
pain. Ask: what if we *don't* hire? Can software or a practice change solve it?
If someone leaves, see how long you can go without replacing them.
> "The right time to hire is when there's more work than you can handle for a sustained period of time. … That's when you're hurting. And that's when it's time to hire, not earlier."

**Pass on great people.** Some companies are addicted to hiring and invent
positions for great people they don't need — who then sit doing busywork that
breeds artificial projects, cost, and complexity.
> "If you don't need someone, you don't need someone. Great has nothing to do with it." Don't fear "the one that got away."

**Strangers at a cocktail party.** Hire too fast and you get a room of polite
strangers who avoid conflict and never say "this idea sucks." That appeasement is
what gets companies in trouble.
> "So hire slowly. It's the only way to avoid winding up at a cocktail party of strangers." You need an environment where people feel safe enough to be honest.

**Resumes are ridiculous.** Resumes are exaggerated, unverifiable, and too easy to
shotgun out by the hundred — a form of spam. The cover letter is a far better
test: you hear the person's actual voice.
> "If someone sends out a resume to three hundred companies, that's a huge red flag." Check the cover letter; trust your gut reaction to the first paragraph.

**Years of irrelevance.** "Five years of experience required" tells you nothing.
After the first six months to a year, the experience curve flattens — the real
difference is dedication, personality, and intelligence.
> "How long someone's been doing it is overrated. What matters is how *well* they've been doing it."

**Forget about formal education.** Plenty of brilliant people don't shine in the
classroom; most top-500 CEOs aren't from Ivy League schools. Too much academia
can even harm (it teaches that longer/stiffer/big-worded writing is better).
> "The pool of great candidates is far bigger than just people who completed college with a stellar GPA." Consider dropouts, low GPAs, community-college and high-school grads.

**Everybody works.** On a small team you need people who *do* the work, not people
who delegate it. Delegators are dead weight who clog the pipes and invent busywork
(and love meetings).
> "With a small team, you need people who are going to *do* work, not delegate work. Everyone's got to be producing. No one can be above the work."

**Hire managers of one.** Managers of one set their own direction, come up with
their own goals, and execute without hand-holding or daily check-ins. Spot them
by backgrounds where they ran or launched something themselves.
> "These people free you from oversight. … You want someone who's capable of building something from scratch and seeing it through."

**Hire great writers.** Among finalists, hire the best writer — regardless of
role. Clear writing is a sign of clear thinking; good writers communicate, make
things easy to understand, and know what to omit.
> "Being a good writer is about more than writing. Clear writing is a sign of clear thinking. … Writing is today's currency for good ideas."

**The best are everywhere.** Don't skip great people because they're far away —
technology makes distance a non-issue. (37signals is spread across many cities
and countries.) Keep a few hours of daily overlap and meet in person a few times a
year.
> "Geography just doesn't matter anymore. Hire the best talent, regardless of where it is."

**Test-drive employees.** Interviews are limited — some people sound like pros but
don't work like pros. Hire candidates for a small paid mini-project (20–40 hours)
to see how they actually work and decide. (BMW's simulated assembly line; Cessna's
role-play.)
> "You need to evaluate the work they can do now, not the work they say they did in the past. … judge them by their actions instead of just their words."

## Damage Control

**Own your bad news.** When something goes wrong, someone will tell the story —
better that it's you. You can't hide anymore; people will post about it. (Contrast
Exxon Valdez's slow, distant response with Ashland Oil's chairman going to the
scene and taking charge within a day.)
> Tips: the message comes from the top; spread it far and wide; "'No comment' is not an option"; apologize like a real person and explain in detail; honestly be concerned about customers — then prove it.

**Speed changes everything.** Getting back to people quickly is the single most
important thing in customer service — it defuses anger fast. Antagonistic
customers flip 180 degrees and become grateful the moment you respond, especially
with a personal answer.
> "Even if you don't have a perfect answer, say something. 'Let me do some research and get back to you' can work wonders."

**How to say you're sorry.** There's no magic script, but there are terrible
ways. The worst is the non-apology ("We're sorry *if* this upset you"). A good
apology accepts responsibility with no conditional "if," gives real detail, and
seeks to make it right. Dissecting "We apologize for any inconvenience this may
have caused": "I'm sorry" beats "we apologize"; a service outage is a crisis, not
an "inconvenience"; "may have" wavers.
> "The number-one principle: How would you feel about the apology if you were on the other end?" You can't apologize your way out of being an ass — trust is built *before* things go wrong.

**Put everyone on the front lines.** Don't shield the people who make the product
from customer feedback. (Restaurants have chefs wait tables; the kids' game
Telephone shows how the message distorts with every person in between.)
> "It's feeling the hurt that really motivates people to fix the problem." Everyone on the team should be connected to customers at least a few times a year. (Craigslist's Craig Newmark still answers support email.)

**Take a deep breath.** When you change anything, knee-jerk reactions pour in.
Resist the urge to panic and revert — people resist change out of habit, negative
voices are louder than positive ones, and the first rocky week usually settles.
> "Make sure you don't foolishly backpedal on a necessary but controversial decision." Let it simmer, show people you're listening, but explain you'll let it ride and see.

## Culture

**You don't create a culture.** Culture isn't installed via mission statements,
slogans, foosball tables, or parties — those are artificial. Real culture is the
by-product of consistent behavior over time.
> "Artificial culture is paint. Real culture is patina." / "Culture is action, not words." If you reward trust, trust gets built in. Like fine scotch, give it time to develop.

**Decisions are temporary.** Don't make up problems you don't have yet ("but what
if…?", "what happens when we scale to 100 million?"). Most things you worry about
never happen, and a small business can change course fast.
> "Don't make up problems you don't have yet. It's not a problem until it's a *real* problem. … Optimize for now and worry about the future later." The ability to change course is an advantage of being small.

**Skip the rock stars.** Ads seeking "rock stars" and "ninjas" are lame. Don't
chase star individuals — build a great *environment*, because there's huge
untapped potential trapped under lame policies and bureaucracy.
> "Rockstar environments develop out of trust, autonomy, and responsibility." Give people the privacy, workspace, and tools they deserve; they're waiting to do great work.

**They're not thirteen.** Treat people like children — require approval and
permission for everything — and you get children's work, plus a culture of
nonthinkers and an "I don't trust you" relationship. Banning YouTube/Facebook
gains nothing.
> "Failing to trust your employees is awfully expensive." You won't get a full eight hours anyway; people need diversions.

**Send people home at 5.** The dream of the lifeless workaholic who sleeps under
the desk is a trap that excuses lousy execution. People with a life outside work
get down to business and use their time wisely.
> "You don't need more hours; you need *better* hours." / "If you want something done, ask the busiest person you know." Don't expect the job to be someone's entire life if you want to keep them.

**Don't scar on the first cut.** The instinct after one thing goes wrong is to
make a policy ("someone wore shorts — we need a dress code!"). Policies are
organizational scar tissue — collective punishment for one person's mistake, and
how bureaucracies are born.
> "Policies are organizational scar tissue. … Don't create a policy because one person did something wrong once. Policies are only meant for situations that come up over and over again."

**Sound like you.** Drop the stiff, fake "professional" corporate-speak — it
sounds like a robot wrote it and talks *at* people, not *to* them. Sounding big
just makes a small company sound ridiculous and sacrifices its real advantage:
communicating simply and directly.
> "Don't be afraid to be you." Talk to customers the way you'd talk to friends; read it out loud; write for one person, not a mob. "Don't use seven words when four will do."

**Four-letter words.** The real four-letter words to avoid in business: *need,
must, can't, easy, just, only, fast* (plus *everyone, no one, always, never*).
They create false black-and-white absolutes that inject conflict and box people
into corners.
> "We need to add this feature now. We can't launch without it. Everyone wants it. It's only one little thing so it will be easy. … Only thirty-six words, but a hundred assumptions. That's a recipe for disaster." ("Easy" is what people call *other* people's jobs.)

**ASAP is poison.** Tacking ASAP onto every request means everything's a top
priority — which means nothing is. It's inflationary; soon nothing gets done
without the ASAP sticker.
> "Reserve your use of emergency language for true emergencies. … For everything else, chill out." Most things won't cause real harm if they're not done this instant.

---

## Conclusion

**Inspiration is perishable.** Ideas are immortal, but inspiration has an
expiration date like fresh fruit. When you're inspired you can do two weeks of
work in twenty-four hours.
> "Inspiration is a now thing. If it grabs you, grab it right back and put it to work."
