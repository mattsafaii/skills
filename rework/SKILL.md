# Rework

*Rework* is a collection of ~85 short essays — most a page or two — each making
one sharp, contrarian argument about how to build a business. The voice is blunt
and quotable on purpose. This skill does two jobs:

1. **Look up** what Rework says on a topic (and quote it accurately).
2. **Apply** the relevant principle(s) to a decision the user is actually facing
   — naming the principle and giving a concrete recommendation for *their*
   situation.

Matt runs two small, solo/lean businesses (Safaii Studio, Zonebrite). Rework's
worldview — small is a real destination, stay independent, profit from day one,
ship, do less — maps closely onto how he already works, so this book is usually
a sharpening tool, not a conversion pitch.

## How to use this

**For a lookup** ("what does Rework say about meetings?"):
- Find the principle(s) in the topic index below, read the entry in
  `references/principles.md`, and answer with the principle name + the key line,
  quoted accurately, plus the supporting reasoning if they want more.

**For an applied decision** ("should I take this retainer client who wants a pile
of custom features?"):
1. Identify which principles bear on the call (usually the 2–3 that actually
   decide it — they interact, but don't list every loosely-related one).
2. Read those entries in `references/principles.md` so you quote and reason from
   the real argument, not a fuzzy memory.
3. Answer in the shape below.

### Shape of a good applied answer

The user wants *"here's my answer, and why, grounded in Rework"* — a
what-would-Fried-&-DHH-do read on their call, not a balanced pros-and-cons list
and not a book report. Three beats:

1. **The call, up front.** A direct recommendation — lean yes, lean no, or "it
   depends on X, and here's where the line falls." Don't bury it.
2. **Why, per Rework.** Name the deciding principle(s) and **quote the actual
   line(s)** from the reference, tied to their situation. The quoting isn't
   decoration — it's how the user picks up the principles over time, so use the
   book's real words, attributed to Rework.
3. **The concrete next step.** What to actually do — phrased for *their*
   situation, in plain terms (avoid filler like "by Monday" unless they gave you
   a real timeline).

Keep it tight. The reasoning and the citations are the value; length is not. If
two principles already decide the call, don't add a third for completeness.

## How to apply it well (and honestly)

- **Present it as Rework's stance, attributed** — "Rework's line here is…" — not
  as settled universal truth. It's a strong, deliberately one-sided point of
  view, and that's its value: it cuts through hand-wringing. But name it as a
  position so the user can disagree.
- **The principles interact and sometimes pull against each other.** "Launch
  now" vs. "build half a product, not a half-assed product"; "say no by default"
  vs. "let your customers outgrow you." Applying Rework well means picking
  *which* principle dominates *this* call and saying why — not reciting all of
  them. When two principles genuinely collide on the user's call, name the
  tension and say which one you'd let win here.
- **It's a 2010 book about software/web businesses.** Most principles travel fine
  to a creative studio or a camera-install business, but some examples are dated
  (Flip cameras, "no PR firm yet") and a few claims are absolutist for effect.
  Don't quote the dated specifics as current; apply the underlying idea.
- **Don't stretch, and know where the book goes silent.** Rework is a book of
  *operating philosophy*, not a reference for the mechanics of running a company.
  It has nothing real to say about legal structure (LLC vs. S-corp), taxes,
  accounting, contract terms, employment law, specific tooling choices, or how to
  implement a feature. When a question is one of those — even though it's a
  "business" question — say the book doesn't cover it rather than generating
  confident, philosophy-flavored noise to fill the gap. Fabricating a "Rework
  would say…" is worse than nothing. Only quote a named principle when it's
  actually in `references/principles.md`.
- **Match Matt's preferences:** short and direct, no fluff. Lead with the call,
  then the principle backing it.

## Failure modes

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---------|-------|-----|
| A quote turns out wrong or paraphrased | Quoted from memory instead of reading the reference | Always pull exact wording from `references/principles.md` before quoting |
| Answer reads like a book report | Every loosely-related principle got listed instead of the deciding ones | Narrow to the 2–3 principles that actually decide this call |
| Recommendation ignores a principle pulling the other way | Applied one principle without checking whether a rival one (e.g. "launch now" vs. "build half a product") also bears on the call | Name the tension explicitly and say which principle wins here, and why |
| Advice is presented as objective truth | Forgot to attribute it as the book's stance | Frame it as "Rework's line here is…", not settled fact |
| A confident "Rework would say…" answer for something the book doesn't cover | Stretched operating philosophy to answer a mechanics question (legal structure, taxes, contracts, tooling) | Say the book doesn't cover it rather than manufacturing a position |
| A dated example gets cited as current advice | Quoted the specific illustration (Flip cameras, "no PR firm yet") instead of the underlying idea | Apply the principle, not the dated specifics |

## Topic index

Map the question to one or more principles, then pull the real wording from
`references/principles.md` (organized in the same section order as below).

**Starting / should I even do this**
- Scratch your own itch — build what you want to use
- Start making something — execution beats the idea
- No time is no excuse — make the time; the perfect moment never comes
- Make a dent in the universe — do something that matters, with urgency
- Enough with "entrepreneurs" — you don't need permission; just be a "starter"
- Inspiration is perishable — when it strikes, act *now*

**Growth, size & ambition**
- Why grow? — small is a destination, not a stepping-stone
- Less mass — avoid commitments that make you slow to change
- Pass on great people — don't hire ahead of real need
- Build a business, not a startup — profit from day one; a no-profit plan is a hobby

**Money & funding**
- Outside money is Plan Z — funding is a noose; avoid it
- You need less than you think — be frugal; great companies start in garages
- Building to flip is building to flop — commitment strategy, not exit strategy

**Planning & decisions**
- Planning is guessing — decide this week, not this year
- Making the call is making progress — decide and move; don't wait for perfect
- Make tiny decisions — small, reversible calls beat big irreversible ones
- Decisions are temporary — don't invent problems you don't have yet
- Learning from mistakes is overrated — build on what worked, not what failed

**Focus, scope & shipping**
- Embrace constraints — limits force creativity
- Build half a product, not a half-assed product — cut ambition, keep quality
- Start at the epicenter — begin with the thing you can't remove
- Ignore the details early on — nail the basics first
- Be a curator — it's what you leave out that matters
- Throw less at the problem — cut back instead of piling on
- Focus on what won't change — build the core on what lasts
- Launch now — ship sooner than feels comfortable
- Quick wins — momentum fuels motivation; ship something every couple weeks
- Your estimates suck — break big things into small ones
- Long lists don't get done — short lists; prioritize visually

**Productivity & work habits**
- Interruption is the enemy of productivity — protect alone-time
- Meetings are toxic — count the true cost; rules if you must meet
- Good enough is fine — find the judo solution; great can come later
- Don't be a hero — sometimes quitting the task is the right move
- Go to sleep — sleep deprivation wrecks judgment and creativity
- Workaholism — more hours ≠ more care or more done
- Tone is in your fingers — content matters, not fancy gear/tools
- Reasons to quit — keep asking *why* you're doing this

**Conviction & point of view**
- Draw a line in the sand — have a stance; expect to turn some people off
- Mission statement impossible — live your stance, don't just post it
- Ignore the real world — "the real world" is an excuse, not a place

**Competitors**
- Don't copy — copying skips the understanding that makes you grow
- Decommoditize your product — make *you* part of the product
- Pick a fight — being the anti-___ differentiates and attracts
- Underdo your competition — win by doing less, better
- Who cares what they're doing? — focus on yourself, not them

**Customers & saying no**
- Say no by default — protect the product; you have to love it most
- Let your customers outgrow you — don't warp the product for one big client
- Don't confuse enthusiasm with priority — let hot new ideas cool off
- Be at-home good — be great in use, not just on the shelf
- Don't write it down — the important requests come back on their own

**Marketing & promotion (no budget)**
- Welcome obscurity — make your early mistakes while no one's watching
- Build an audience — earn attention so you don't have to buy it
- Out-teach your competition — teach what you know; big companies won't
- Emulate chefs — share your recipes openly
- Go behind the scenes — show how the work gets made
- Nobody likes plastic flowers — show your flaws; be real
- Sell your by-products — there's something you make without realizing it
- Press releases are spam — pitch a person, not a blast
- Forget about the Wall Street Journal — niche/trade media beats mass media
- Drug dealers get it right — give a free taste of something addictive
- Marketing is not a department — everything you do is marketing
- The myth of the overnight sensation — slow, measured growth; skip the PR firm

**Hiring & team**
- Do it yourself first — do the job before you hire for it
- Hire when it hurts — hire to kill real pain, not for pleasure
- Strangers at a cocktail party — hire slowly so people can be honest
- Resumes are ridiculous — read the cover letter instead
- Years of irrelevance — how *well* they've done it, not how long
- Forget about formal education — the great-candidate pool is bigger than top GPAs
- Everybody works — no delegators; everyone produces
- Hire managers of one — self-directed people who don't need oversight
- Hire great writers — clear writing signals clear thinking
- The best are everywhere — hire remote; geography doesn't matter
- Test-drive employees — judge them on real work, not interviews

**Damage control & customer service**
- Own your bad news — tell the story before someone else does
- Speed changes everything — a fast reply defuses an angry customer
- How to say you're sorry — a real apology, no "we apologize for any inconvenience"
- Put everyone on the front lines — keep the makers in touch with customers
- Take a deep breath — ride out the knee-jerk reaction to any change

**Culture, policy & language**
- You don't create a culture — culture is the by-product of consistent behavior
- Skip the rock stars — build a great environment, not a roster of stars
- They're not thirteen — don't treat employees like children
- Send people home at 5 — want people with a life; better hours, not more
- Don't scar on the first cut — don't make a policy for a one-off mistake
- Sound like you — drop fake corporate professionalism; talk like a person
- Four-letter words — watch *need, must, can't, easy, just, only, fast*
- ASAP is poison — save emergency language for real emergencies

See `references/principles.md` for the full text of every principle, grouped by
the book's own sections (Takedowns, Go, Progress, Productivity, Competitors,
Evolution, Promotion, Hiring, Damage Control, Culture, Conclusion).
